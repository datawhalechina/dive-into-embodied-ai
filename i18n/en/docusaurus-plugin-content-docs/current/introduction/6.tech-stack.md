# 6. Technology stack

To enable a robot to carry out diverse tasks and solve real-world problems with human-like flexibility and intelligence, no single model or algorithm is enough. Much like human action relies on sensing, high-level reasoning, and fine motor coordination working together, building a working embodied AI robot connects two groups of technologies into one stack:

- **Runtime loop (see clearly, decide smartly, move reliably)**: **[Perception and localization](#perception)** captures the 3D environment and robot state, **[Foundation models and policies](#foundation-models)** interprets instructions and generates actions, and **[Planning and motion control](#motion-control)** turns intent into safe, compliant, high-rate physical motion.
- **Engineering and physical foundation (train cheaply, learn well, run fast, stay within physical limits)**: **[Simulation engineering](#simulation)** enables scalable virtual trial-and-error, **[Data engineering](#data-engineering)** turns human demonstrations into clean training sets, **[Deployment engineering](#deployment)** connects onboard communication and real-time inference, and **[Hardware foundations](#hardware)** defines the mechanical, actuation, and bus constraints.

This chapter begins with an **overview** of why these layers must work together, then unpacks all seven modules by the practical problems they solve, linking directly to the [foundations](/docs/foundations/intro) and [projects](/docs/practices/intro).

## Overview \{#overview}

<TechStackDiagram />

Take a single everyday instruction: **“Go to the kitchen and put the red cup on the table onto the plate.”** Why can a single model rarely take camera pixels straight to motor currents and handle everything on its own? Because one sentence triggers fundamentally different constraints:

- **Different time scales**: understanding the instruction and planning steps can run every few hundred milliseconds (a few Hz), cameras and sensors typically update at tens of Hz, while obstacle avoidance, contact force regulation, and balance often require stable closed-loop control at tens to thousands of Hz.
- **Different data sources and safety boundaries**: web-scale text and images provide semantic commonsense, whereas 3D distances depend on calibration and geometry, safety requires deterministic dynamics and limits, and physical trial-and-error is slow and expensive.

End-to-end VLAs and fast–slow dual systems are steadily merging intermediate stages, yet high-level generalization, low-level real-time control, and engineering infrastructure still play distinct roles. The table below summarizes what each module solves:

General prerequisites across these modules are mainly **Python** (algorithms, simulation, and data processing), **C++** (real-time control, ROS 2, and low-level deployment), **Linux**, **PyTorch**, and basic linear algebra and coordinate transformations. You do not need to master everything upfront—start from one concrete problem and fill in gaps as you go.

## 6.1 Perception and localization \{#perception}

**Problem to solve**: Where is the cup in 3D space and what is its pose? Where has the robot moved, and is its body tilting or slipping?

Grasping, manipulation, legged locomotion, and mobile navigation all depend on consistent coordinate frames and trustworthy sensor observations. Perception is not an add-on to navigation; it is the shared entry point of every action loop.

### Calibration and 3D vision \{#perception-vision}

- **Sensors and calibration**: learn about RGB and depth cameras, lidar, IMUs, and tactile sensors, along with camera intrinsics and extrinsics, hand-eye calibration, time synchronization, and coordinate projection. Start with [sensor calibration and sim2real](/docs/foundations/perception/sensor-calibration-sim2real) and [sensor projection](/docs/foundations/perception/sensor-projection).
- **3D geometry and object representations**: convert pixels, depth maps, or point clouds into segmentation, 3D geometry, and object 6D poses, answering where targets and obstacles sit in space to inform grasping, manipulation, and obstacle avoidance.

### State estimation and SLAM \{#localization}

- **Proprioception and state estimation**: fuse joint encoders, IMUs, foot contact, and odometry to estimate body pose, velocity, and drift so the robot knows whether it is losing balance. Start with [odometry, timestamps, and state estimation](/docs/foundations/perception/odo-timestamps-state-estimation) and [tf2](/docs/foundations/robotics-and-ros2/tf2).
- **Localization and mapping (SLAM)**: estimate the robot's position while building a map of an unknown environment; combined with semantic information, this also supports open-world navigation toward language goals.

Current material covers calibration, projection, state estimation, and coordinate trees. The [mapping and navigation project](/docs/practices/mobile-manipulation/navigation-basics/placeholder) and [vision-language navigation project](/docs/practices/mobile-manipulation/vision-language-nav/placeholder) are still planned.

## 6.2 Foundation models and policies \{#foundation-models}

**Problem to solve**: How does the robot ground “the red cup” in the image, understand the task goal, and learn continuous actions from human demonstrations or interaction instead of hand-coding rules for every new object and layout?

This layer handles high-level understanding and data-driven decision making. For “put the red cup on the plate,” a VLM interprets the image and instruction, imitation learning or a VLA generates actions directly from observations, and a world model predicts how actions will change the environment. These capabilities can work together inside one system.

### VLM: vision and language \{#vlm}

A vision-language model processes images and text together for scene description, question answering, target grounding, and task reasoning. Start by learning how images become visual features and how language models process sequences, then study how the two modalities are fused.

Key modules include Transformers, language models, visual encoders, image-text alignment, and multimodal fusion. The model must connect “red cup” in the instruction with the right object in the image to provide semantic guidance for downstream actions.

Continue learning: [Multimodal model foundations](/docs/foundations/vlm/intro) → [Visual encoders and alignment](/docs/foundations/vlm/visual_encoders_and_alignment) → [Multimodal fusion and VLMs](/docs/foundations/vlm/multimodal_fusion_and_vlm).

### Imitation learning and VLA \{#vla}

For contact-rich manipulation tasks that are hard to program with explicit rules, policies can be learned directly from demonstrated observations and actions:

- **Imitation learning (IL)**: closes the loop from images and proprioceptive state to action sequences without requiring language instructions, making it a classic entry point for learned manipulation. ACT predicts a chunk of future actions at once, while Diffusion Policy generates multimodal action sequences through a diffusion process. Start with [imitation learning](/docs/foundations/rl-for-robotics/imitation-learning), then explore [ACT](/docs/foundations/vla/act_action_chunking) and [Diffusion Policy](/docs/foundations/vla/diffusion_policy).
- **Vision-language-action models (VLA)**: combine pretrained multimodal understanding with action generation, taking images, language instructions, and robot state to output joint targets, end-effector poses, or continuous action chunks. Focus on action representations, action chunking, diffusion or flow-matching generation, how model outputs enter the control loop, and reinforcement learning post-training from interaction outcomes. OpenVLA and π0 illustrate different designs.

Continue learning: [From VLM to VLA](/docs/foundations/vlm/vlm_to_vla) → [VLA series introduction](/docs/foundations/vla/vla-intro) → [OpenVLA](/docs/foundations/vla/openvla_engineering) / [π0](/docs/foundations/vla/pi0_foundation_model).

Practice: [ACT bimanual training](/docs/practices/vla/act) walks through data, training, and evaluation for learned manipulation; [π₀.₅ + RECAP reproduction](/docs/practices/vla/recap) covers data preparation, value models, policy fine-tuning, and simulation evaluation for VLAs.

### World models \{#world-models}

World models learn how an environment changes over time and in response to actions. Given current observations and candidate actions, they predict future states, images, or latent representations to support planning, policy learning, or offline evaluation.

Study action-conditioned prediction, latent dynamics, video prediction, and compounding error over multiple steps. Understand not only how predictions help select actions, but also how to check whether a model remains trustworthy in new settings.

Continue learning: [World model introduction](/docs/foundations/world-model/intro) → [World model survey and technical approaches](/docs/foundations/world-model/survey-and-map). Current coverage focuses on concepts, methods, and research surveys.

A practical starting order for this layer is “multimodal foundations → VLM → imitation learning / VLA,” followed by world models in the context of planning or policy learning.

## 6.3 Planning and motion control \{#motion-control}

**Problem to solve**: Once perception locates the cup and obstacles and the high-level layer sets a target pose or desired motion, how does the arm reach smoothly without colliding, regulate force without crushing the cup, and keep a mobile base or legs stable over uneven ground?

This layer turns high-level targets into high-rate, safe joint or wheel commands that obey physical constraints. Even when an end-to-end policy sits above it, planning, feedback control, and force control remain essential for precision and safety.

### Kinematics and planning \{#planning}

- **Arm kinematics and trajectory planning**: forward kinematics computes end-effector pose from joint angles, while inverse kinematics solves for joint configurations; motion planning adds reachability, smooth trajectories, and collision constraints. Start with [coordinate transformations and kinematics](/docs/foundations/robotics-and-ros2/kinematics_transform), then study [MoveIt 2](/docs/foundations/robotics-and-ros2/moveit2_basics).
- **Mobile path planning and navigation execution**: search for global paths on a map, generate executable velocity commands around dynamic obstacles under motion constraints, and handle recovery behaviors. Nav2 is widely used to organize planning, control, and recovery; vision-language navigation (VLN) further incorporates natural-language goals and semantics.

Practice: the [MoveIt 2 simulation project](/docs/foundations/robotics-and-ros2/moveit2_project) connects motion planning with execution.

### Feedback, dynamics, and force \{#model-control}

- **Feedback control**: continuously corrects commands from the error between desired and current state. Learn PID / PD, sampling rates, limits, and stability in [feedback and PID](/docs/foundations/controllers/feedback-pid).
- **Dynamics and optimization-based control**: use robot dynamics models to predict motion and solve for control signals under contact and actuator constraints via LQR, trajectory tracking, MPC, and whole-body control (WBC); engineering workflows commonly rely on libraries such as Pinocchio and CasADi. Follow the [controller course](/docs/foundations/controllers/intro) chapter by chapter.
- **Contact and force control**: grasping, wiping, and assembly involve contact forces and deformation, requiring a balance between position tracking, compliance, and contact force rather than pushing rigidly into objects. Continue with [impedance and force control](/docs/foundations/controllers/impedance-force-control).

### RL locomotion and control \{#rl-control}

For contact-rich tasks such as quadruped, biped, and humanoid walking where complete analytical models are hard to handcraft, define observations, actions, and rewards so policies learn through repeated simulation trials, then bridge to hardware with reward design, domain randomization, and actuator modeling. Start with [MDPs](/docs/foundations/rl-for-robotics/mdp) and [PPO](/docs/foundations/rl-for-robotics/ppo).

Practice: [Build a quadruped from scratch](/docs/practices/quadruped/cs123/intro) connects PD control, kinematics, and gaits; [MicroDuck RL](/docs/practices/humanoid/microduck-rl) demonstrates GPU-parallel bipedal policy training and evaluation.

## 6.4 Simulation engineering \{#simulation}

**Problem to solve**: Real robots are expensive, run strictly in real time, and take time to repair after crashes. How can algorithms be trained and evaluated safely, in parallel, and repeatably before touching hardware?

Start with a minimal scene, then incrementally add the robot model, sensors, controllers, and training tasks.

- **Robot and scene descriptions**: understand how joints, links, inertia, collision shapes, and actuators form a model through [URDF](/docs/foundations/robotics-and-ros2/urdf_and_rsp) and [MJCF](/docs/foundations/simulation/mjcf-element).
- **Physics and sensor simulation**: learn how time steps, contact, friction, and sensor settings affect outcomes, and build environments with [MuJoCo](/docs/foundations/simulation/mujoco) or [Isaac Sim](/docs/foundations/simulation/isaac_sim) (along with parallel learning frameworks such as Isaac Lab).
- **Training environments and evaluation**: package observations, actions, rewards, and resets into a standard interface, then study parallel training, domain randomization, and repeatable evaluation. Start with the environment interface in [Gymnasium](/docs/foundations/simulation/gymnasium).

Practice: [MuJoCo simulation introduction](/docs/practices/robot-arm/mujoco-arm-pick-place) creates and runs an environment; [MicroDuck RL](/docs/practices/humanoid/microduck-rl) shows a complete training workflow.

## 6.5 Data engineering \{#data-engineering}

**Problem to solve**: Imitation learning and VLAs depend heavily on demonstration trajectories. How do you collect, inspect, clean, and organize sensor observations, actions, and task metadata into reusable training datasets?

- **Teleoperation and collection**: understand how operator inputs map to robot actions while synchronized multi-view images, joint states, and timestamps are recorded.
- **Data organization and quality**: understand episode boundaries, task labels, action units, control rates, and calibration metadata, and detect dropped frames, timestamp drift, and abnormal actions.
- **Training data preparation**: study normalization, training / validation splits, dataset versioning, and batch loading, and how data distributions shape policy performance.

Continue learning: [Embodied AI datasets](./resources/datasets.md) explains how to read public dataset specifications; the [LeRobot course](/docs/practices/robot-arm/data-collection/lerobot-course) introduces the robot learning toolchain.

Practice: the [SO-101 hardware tutorial](/docs/practices/robot-arm/data-collection/so101-lerobot-real) connects devices, control, and data workflows; [ACT bimanual training](/docs/practices/vla/act) shows how demonstrations enter training and evaluation.

## 6.6 Deployment engineering \{#deployment}

**Problem to solve**: Once training code works on a workstation GPU, how do you connect it to real sensors and actuators on a robot with low latency, reliable communication, and safety safeguards?

- **ROS 2 and system communication**: learn nodes, topics, services, actions, DDS communication, and launch configuration so modules exchange state and commands reliably. Start with [ROS 2 engineering basics](/docs/foundations/robotics-and-ros2/ros2_engineering_basics).
- **Model inference and asynchronous execution**: bridge the rate gap between slower large models and fast low-level controllers by understanding inference latency, control rates, action chunking, and asynchronous execution in [RTC real-time execution](/docs/foundations/vla/rtc_realtime_execution); tools such as ONNX Runtime, TensorRT, and edge platforms like Jetson support onboard acceleration.
- **Control interfaces and safety debugging**: map policy outputs to position, velocity, or torque commands while checking units, timing, velocity/torque limits, emergency stops, and fault states. Start with [controller integration](/docs/foundations/controllers/integration).

Practice: use the [MoveIt 2 simulation project](/docs/foundations/robotics-and-ros2/moveit2_project) to understand system integration, then follow the [SO-101 hardware tutorial](/docs/practices/robot-arm/data-collection/so101-lerobot-real) for device connectivity and a minimal control loop.

## 6.7 Hardware foundations \{#hardware}

**Problem to solve**: Can the robot's structure, motors, power system, and communication buses actually deliver the speeds, torques, and trajectories an algorithm commands—and when hardware fails, how do you tell an algorithm issue from a physical limit?

- **Mechanics and actuators**: learn about links, joints, transmissions, motors, and gearboxes, and understand degrees of freedom, workspace, payload, backlash, and stiffness.
- **Electronics and motor drives**: learn about power supplies, sensor circuits, encoders, and drives, and how current, velocity, and position loops relate to one another.
- **Embedded systems and communication**: learn about MCUs, real-time tasks, and buses such as CAN, including how host commands reach actuators and how state returns reliably.

Start with [URDF and robot models](/docs/foundations/robotics-and-ros2/urdf_and_rsp), then use the [quadruped course](/docs/practices/quadruped/cs123/intro) to connect modeling with control. [Mechanical design](/docs/foundations/hardware/placeholder) and [CAN and MCU communication](/docs/foundations/communication/can-mcu) are currently placeholders.

## Module combinations by task \{#task-combinations}

Connecting back to the skills introduced in [Chapter 3](./3.tasks-and-skills.md), real tasks combine subsets of these layers according to their needs:

<div className="wide-table">

| Real-world task | Sensing (6.1) | Brain (6.2) | Cerebellum (6.3) | Common engineering support (6.4–6.7) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Grasping and manipulation** (arms / hands) | Hand-eye calibration, depth & 6D pose, tactile | VLM, imitation learning (ACT / DP), VLA | FK/IK, MoveIt 2 planning, impedance & force control | Teleoperation data, LeRobot, hardware safety limits |
| **Legged and humanoid locomotion** | IMU, joint encoders, terrain / elevation sensing | (High-level task or velocity commands) | PID / PD, MPC / WBC, RL locomotion & sim2real | GPU-parallel simulation, domain randomization, high-rate deployment |
| **Mobile navigation** | Camera / lidar calibration, odometry, SLAM | Optional VLM / semantic goal understanding (VLN) | Global & local path planning (Nav2), base control | ROS 2 / tf2 integration, log replay debugging |

</div>

In industry, engineering teams often group adjacent modules by task—for example, manipulation engineers commonly span imitation learning / VLAs, motion planning, and force control, while navigation engineers connect calibration, SLAM, and path planning.

## Where to start \{#next}

Pick one concrete problem to try first—such as calibrating a camera and projecting coordinates, reproducing an ACT manipulation policy, or making a simulated quadruped walk steadily. Read the matching theory chapters, complete an existing experiment, record its inputs, outputs, and failure modes, and gradually connect adjacent modules into a full system.
